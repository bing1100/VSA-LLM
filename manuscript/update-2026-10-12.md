# VSA-LLM results update (12 October 2026)

A simple summary of every result so far. Each finding has the same parts:
- **TL;DR**: the answer in one sentence.
- **Experiment**: what we ran.
- **Method**: how it works.
- **Key results**: the numbers that back it.
- **Implication**: what it means.
- **Gap / fix**: what is still missing and how to close it.
- **Fair?**: whether the comparison was a fair one.

Sources are the committed reports (listed at the end). The earlier plain-language status is [status-2026-10-08.md](status-2026-10-08.md).

## How to read the numbers

- **The method (C5).** We add a vector to a pretrained language model at the last token of each multi-token term. The vector is built ("composed") from the term's ontology entry: its relations, such as *is-a*, *contains element* or *treats*. Composition draws on a small shared dictionary of related-concept and relation vectors, which is trained together with the model.
- **The models compared** (unless stated, all are trained on the same text for the same 50M tokens):
  - **P0**: the original pretrained model, not trained further.
  - **C0′**: the model fine-tuned on the domain text without the method. This is the fair baseline.
  - **C2**: a free lookup table with one learned vector per term and no ontology. It has the same parameter budget as C5.
- **Term categories:**
  - **Held-out terms**: kept out of the method's training on purpose. At test time their vector is built from the ontology alone ("zero-shot").
  - **Unseen terms**: in the ontology, but never in the training text.
  - **Rare terms**: seen 1–9 times in training.
- **Numbers.** Unless stated, a number is the change in next-token prediction loss on the tokens right after a term, relative to C0′. **Negative means better.** Values are means over 3 training seeds, with 95% intervals in brackets. `*` means statistically significant after correction for multiple tests. **Seed 1** marks a single-run result.
- **Hosts** are SmolLM2-135M and SmolLM2-360M (fully fine-tuned) and Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B (LoRA).
- **Tracks** (the test domains):

| Track | Text | Ontology | New to the model? |
|---|---|---|---|
| T5 | synthetic company glossary (invented words, templated text) | the glossary's own entries | completely (invented) |
| T4 | real chemistry: ChEBI entries and PubMed abstracts | ChEBI | fairly new (plain fine-tuning lowers loss after these terms by 40–56%) |
| T7 | PubMed 2025–26 abstracts mentioning 2026 MeSH names | MeSH 2026 supplementary records | barely (7–10%) |
| T1-open | PubMed | MeSH | known (5–8%) |
| WordNet | general web text | WordNet | known (< 1%) |
| T1c | clinical notes (MIMIC-III, licensed) | SNOMED CT | the note style is new (37–41%) |

---

## Bottom line (12 October)

1. **The method works where words are truly new:** 9–20% on invented words, across four models.
2. **On real text it helps a little (≈ 0.5–2%), and only where the words are new to the model.** On words the model knows it does nothing. Clinical notes and chemistry show gains after long terms only.
3. **On real text, what helps is sharing building blocks between terms,** not the binding math and not (much) the truth of each term's relations. Other ways of encoding the same entry (TransE, definitions) do as well.
4. **Zero-shot vectors for unseen words help on real text, but hurt for words the model already learned from text.** The clean test (T7-ROOD) is ≈ 20–21 Oct.
5. **"VSA" earns its place as a readable store:** decoding it into the prompt lets models resolve roles they otherwise cannot (0.50 → 0.78; 0.55 → 0.88 on chemistry, seed 1). It does not earn it in language-model loss.
6. **No claims for:** quantization robustness, self-learning speed, editing on real text, or one-shot reading on real text.

## E9 — adding the method to pretrained models

### E9-1. On invented words the method works

- **TL;DR:** on a vocabulary the model has never seen, the composed vector lowers prediction loss after held-out terms by 9–13% (SmolLM2) and 18–20% (Qwen3).
- **Experiment:**
  - T5 synthetic glossary, with 360 held-out terms that are never linked in training.
  - SmolLM2-135M and 360M at 3 seeds; Qwen3-0.6B and 1.7B (LoRA) at 2 seeds.
  - C5 is compared with C0′ and C2 on the same text and the same tokens.
- **Method:** the model is fine-tuned on glossary documents while the composed vectors are added at each linked term. At test time, held-out terms get a vector composed from their ontology entry, which the method has never trained on.
- **Key results** (after held-out terms, vs C0′):

| Host | Change in loss |
|---|---|
| SmolLM2-360M | **−13.3% [−14.1, −12.4]*** |
| SmolLM2-135M | **−9.2% [−10.0, −8.4]*** |
| Qwen3-1.7B (2 seeds) | −17.7% [−18.6, −16.8]* |
| Qwen3-0.6B (2 seeds) | −20.2% [−21.3, −19.1]* |

  - Every seed shows the gain.
  - After unseen terms: −6.6%. After rare terms: −6.0% (360M).
  - Text without linked terms is unchanged (−0.03%).
  - The free table C2 is *worse* than plain fine-tuning on these terms, so the gain comes from composition, not from extra capacity.
- **Implication:** the method does what it was designed to do when the vocabulary is truly new.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The text is generated from the same ontology the method reads, so these sizes are an upper bound (see E9-8).
  - Qwen3 seed 3 and the Qwen3.5 hosts are queued.
- **Fair?** Yes within the track: same data, same tokens and same trainable host for C5, C0′ and C2; C2 is matched on parameters; 3 seeds. But the track favours the method, since its text is templated from the ontology. Treat it as the best case.

### E9-2. On real text the gain is small and follows how new the words are

- **TL;DR:** on real text the method helps by 0.4–2.3% where the words are fairly new (chemistry), about 0.5% where they are barely new, and not at all where the model already knows them.
- **Experiment:** the same recipe on five more tracks.
  - T4 (ChEBI/PubMed chemistry, 360M, 3 seeds; 135M seed 1).
  - T7 (2026 MeSH names, 360M, 3 seeds).
  - T1-open and WordNet (both hosts, seed 1).
  - T1c clinical (both hosts, seed 1; see E9-11).
- **Method:** the same as E9-1. A "novelty index" measures how new the words are: how much plain fine-tuning (C0′) lowers loss after the terms, compared with the untouched model (P0).
- **Key results** (360M; after held-out / unseen / rare / long (3+ subtoken) terms):

| Track | Novelty (C0′ vs P0) | Method gain (C5 vs C0′) |
|---|---|---|
| T5 synthetic | −74 to −81% | −13.3 / −6.6 / −6.0 / −7.6% |
| T4 chemistry (3 seeds) | −40 to −56% | −0.40 / **−2.29** / −0.91 / −1.09% (all *) |
| T7 2026 MeSH (3 seeds) | −7 to −10% | **+0.48** (worse) / — / −0.50 / −0.57% |
| T1-open (seed 1) | −5 to −8% | ≈ 0 everywhere |
| WordNet (seed 1) | < 1% | ≈ 0 everywhere |

- **Implication:** the method fills a gap where the model lacks the word. On known words, fine-tuning alone does the job. The paper's claim must be scoped to "vocabulary new to the model", with natural-text sizes of about 1–2%.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - Only two real tracks have 3 seeds.
  - T7-ROOD (≈ 20–21 Oct) and T8 Wikidata (≈ 24 Oct) add real tracks.
  - Qwen3-1.7B on T4, T7, T7-ROOD and T8 is queued (a modern host on real text).
  - T4 135M seeds 2–3 are queued (≈ 14 Oct).
- **Fair?**
  - Within each track, yes (same recipe and test set).
  - Across tracks, only roughly. "Held-out" means different things: on T5 the words never appear in the text, while on T4 and T7 they appear unlinked (E9-6).
  - Five tracks are too few to be a dose–response curve.

### E9-3. The binding operator is not what helps

- **TL;DR:** replacing the vector-symbolic binding (HRR) with simpler operations gives the same or better loss, so the "VSA math" is not the source of the gain.
- **Experiment:** C5 with the binding swapped, one change at a time, on T5 and T4 (360M, 3 seeds):
  - fixed random binding (C5rf);
  - no binding, a plain sum of related words (C5ut);
  - translation, relation added instead of bound (C5tr).

  The from-scratch operator screen is in E4 below.
- **Method:** keep everything else identical and compare loss.
- **Key results:**
  - **T5, after held-out terms:** no binding equals HRR (+0.2%, n.s.); translation beats HRR (−17.2% vs C0′, against −13.3%); fixed random binding is 5.2% worse.
  - **T4:** all four operators tie (C5 − C5rf / C5ut n.s.; C5tr within ±0.14%).
- **Implication:** describe the mechanism as *compositional parameter sharing*, not as symbolic binding (project rule 7). "VSA" earns its place only in explicit read-out (E12, below).
- **Gap / fix:** none for loss. A word-order-aware operator is being tested in the binding readout arms (queued).
- **Fair?** Yes: same recipe, seeds and test set; only the operator changes.

### E9-4. On real text, composing has no edge over other per-term vectors

- **TL;DR:** on chemistry and 2026 MeSH text, a TransE knowledge-graph vector or an encoded definition of the same ontology entry does as well as the composed vector.
- **Experiment:** same-site alternatives on T5, T4 and T7 (360M, 3 seeds), each with a projector matched on parameters:
  - the mean of the term's subtoken embeddings (C6m);
  - the frozen model's encoding of the entry written as text (C6d, "definition encoder");
  - a TransE embedding of the entry (C6g).
- **Method:** each alternative gives one fixed vector per term, which is fed through a small trained projector at the same position.
- **Key results:**
  - **T5:** composition beats all three alternatives by 10.8–15.3% after held-out terms.
  - **T4:**
    - TransE ties composition on every stratum.
    - The definition encoder is *ahead* after held-out terms (C5 − C6d +0.17% [+0.05, +0.29]*) and ties after rare and unseen terms.
    - Only the subtoken mean is clearly beaten.
  - **T7:** after held-out terms C5 is behind both the definition encoder and the shuffled-frames arm (official report).
- **Implication:** on real text the claim is "information from the ontology entry at the term's position helps", not "composition is better". The composition-specific claim is synthetic-only (pre-registered framing rule, triggered).
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The TransE comparison favours TransE: it was trained with the held-out entries in its graph (transductive).
  - The fair re-test is **C6gi**: TransE trained *without* the held-out entries, then given a vector afterwards, on T7-ROOD (queued, ≈ 20–21 Oct).
  - In-context definitions are also queued (≈ 14 Oct).
- **Fair?** Mostly. The alternatives get the same frame information and a projector matched on parameters. TransE had an unfair advantage on held-out terms, which C6gi removes.

### E9-5. What helps on real text is sharing building blocks between terms

- **TL;DR:** a code built from the *wrong* relations (shuffled between terms) keeps most of the gain on chemistry text and still beats a free per-term table, so most of the benefit is sharing vectors across terms, not the truth of each term's relations.
- **Experiment:** on T4 and T7 (360M, 3 seeds), compare C5, C5sh (each term reads another term's relations, shuffled) and C2 (free table).
- **Method:** in C5sh every term still gets a consistent vector made from shared building blocks, but from the wrong entry. The free table gives each term its own unshared vector.
- **Key results** (after long terms, vs C0′):

| Track | C5 (correct relations) | C5sh (shuffled relations) | C2 (free table) |
|---|---|---|---|
| T4 | −1.09% | −0.89% | −0.23% |
| T7 | −0.57% | −0.56% | −0.46% |

  - On T4 the relations' own content is worth −0.20%* (C5 − C5sh); on T7 it is ≈ 0.
  - On T5, by contrast, shuffling destroys the gain: +4.6%, worse than no method.
- **Implication:** on real text, the method works mainly as structured parameter sharing. The ontology's actual facts add a smaller part (T4) or nothing measurable (T7).
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The free table's vectors are narrow: 34 numbers per term on T4, against 256 for the composed vector.
  - The **C2w** control (free table at the composed width) is queued (≈ 14 Oct). If C2w catches up with C5sh, the right wording becomes "a wide enough per-term vector", not "sharing".
- **Fair?** Partly. C2 has the same total parameters as C5 but much narrower rows on T4, which may handicap it. C2w fixes this.

### E9-6. The term's own vector is used, but it hurts for words the model already learned unlinked

- **TL;DR:** inside a trained model, a term's own vector clearly helps for terms absent from training, but for held-out terms that appear in the training text unlinked, adding any vector is a small net cost.
- **Experiment:** a pre-registered "frame swap" on finished models (C5, 360M, 3 seeds; T5, T4, T7; T1 and WordNet seed 1). At evaluation only, each term's vector is replaced by:
  - another term's vector ("other");
  - nothing ("empty");
  - an average vector ("mean").
- **Method:** no retraining. If loss rises when a term's own vector is replaced, the model is using that vector.
- **Key results** (variant − own; positive = the own vector helps):

| Track · terms | other | empty |
|---|---|---|
| T5 · held-out | +24.4%* | +28.7%* |
| T4 · unseen (never in training text) | +1.33%* | +1.60%* |
| T4 · rare | +0.75%* | +0.79%* |
| T4 · held-out | +0.19%* | **−0.19%*** (no vector is better) |
| T7 · held-out | −0.01% (n.s.) | **−0.44%*** (no vector is better) |
| T7 · rare | +0.50%* | +0.66%* |

  - T4 and T7 held-out terms were chosen among frequent words. They stay in the training text unlinked, so the model learns them as plain tokens, and the injected vector at test time is new to it.
  - C5's −0.40% held-out gain on T4 therefore comes from the host weights trained alongside the method, not from the zero-shot vector.
  - Across all linked terms, removing the vectors costs more than the method's whole gain: the model co-adapts to them. "No vector" therefore measures dependence, not benefit.
- **Implication:**
  - Zero-shot composition does work on natural text for words the model never read (T4 unseen).
  - Injecting a vector for a word the model already learned without one does not help.
  - Deployment rule: inject only for terms the model has not already learned from text.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The clean zero-shot test is T7-ROOD, where held-out names are removed from the training text (≈ 20–21 Oct).
  - A training fix, link dropout (C5ld: inject only 70% of the time in training), is queued on T7 (≈ 14 Oct).
- **Fair?** Yes as a diagnostic: pre-registered, the same model and windows, and "own" reproduces the official evaluation exactly. It measures use inside a model, not benefit over C0′.

### E9-7. T7's predicted gain failed after held-out names

- **TL;DR:** on new 2026 MeSH names the method *hurts* after held-out names (+0.48%), against the pre-registered prediction of a gain at least as large as chemistry's.
- **Experiment:** T7: PubMed 2025–26 abstracts that mention MeSH 2026 supplementary-concept names (360M, 3 seeds; P0, C0′, C2, C5, C5sh, C6d).
- **Method:** the same as E9-1. Held-out names are composed zero-shot.
- **Key results:**
  - After held-out names: C5 − C0′ +0.48% [+0.35, +0.62]* (worse). After rare names −0.50%*; after long names −0.57%*.
  - Every arm that injects a term-specific vector at held-out positions is worse than C0′: shuffled +0.31%, definition encoder +0.26%.
  - The free table, which injects its average row there, is neutral (−0.02%).
  - Two causes:
    - the held-out names appear unlinked in 22.7% of the training abstracts;
    - the names are barely new to the model (novelty index only −7 to −10%).
- **Implication:**
  - The prediction failed and is recorded as failed.
  - The real-text zero-shot claim must exclude terms already present in the training text.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - **T7-ROOD** drops those abstracts and refills the budget, so that held-out names are truly unseen.
  - It is pre-registered with the prediction that C5 helps after held-out terms.
  - It runs with the C5sh, C6d and C6gi controls and the in-context baseline (≈ 20–21 Oct).
- **Fair?** As a test of the method, no: the confound was built into the track. That is exactly why T7-ROOD exists.

### E9-8. Copying related words explains most of the synthetic gain, little of the real one

- **TL;DR:** on the synthetic glossary, most of the gain after rare and unseen terms comes from predicting words that appear in the term's entry ("copying"). On real text, copying carries at most half after unseen terms and almost nothing elsewhere.
- **Experiment:** the target tokens after each term were split into "filler" tokens (words of the term's ontology entry) and the rest (`e9_rescore`).
- **Method:** compare the gain on the two groups.
- **Key results** (share of the gain that falls on filler tokens):

| Track | after held-out | after unseen | after rare | after long |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T5 | ⅓ | 86–90% | 86–90% | 75% |
| T4 (360M, 3 seeds) | 1% | 51% | 25% | 12% |
| T7 (3 seeds) | ≈ 0% | — | ≈ 0% | ≈ 0% |

  On T5, two thirds of the held-out gain is on non-filler tokens (−19.4%): templated, type-specific continuations.
- **Implication:**
  - The synthetic sizes are inflated by templated text.
  - The real-text gains are not copying.
- **Gap / fix:** none needed. A stricter "no copy" stratum (frequency-bias analysis) is queued (≈ 17 Oct).
- **Fair?** Yes: the same tokens, split by a fixed rule.

### E9-9. 4-bit quantization: the gain survives, but its direction depends on track and quantizer

- **TL;DR:** after 4-bit compression the method's advantage roughly survives, but it shrinks on chemistry, is kept on the synthetic glossary, and grows on T7 only under the crudest rounding. There is no general "more robust when quantized" claim.
- **Experiment:**
  - 4-bit and 8-bit weight-only compression of each trained model, with the output head and input embedding kept at 16 bit.
  - 3 seeds on T5, T4 and T7 (360M).
  - Quantizers: round-to-nearest (RTN, the simplest), HQQ, NF4, GPTQ and AWQ.
  - Variants with the method's channel switched off, and with the embedding quantized too.
- **Method:** measure whether the C5 − C0′ gap changes from full precision to 4-bit ("difference in differences", DiD; negative = the advantage grows).
- **Key results:**
  - **INT8** is free everywhere.
  - **T5:**
    - RTN keeps 0.58× (360M) to 1.14× (135M) of the held-out advantage.
    - GPTQ keeps it fully (DiD −0.001).
    - The extra robustness on ordinary text sits in the host weights.
  - **T4:**
    - INT4 keeps 0.65–0.94×.
    - GPTQ loses about 70% of the held-out advantage (+0.0040*).
    - The loss sits in the host weights.
  - **T7:**
    - Under RTN the gain after seen terms grows 1.6× (after rare terms −0.0077*).
    - With the channel off this growth disappears (−0.0003), and under GPTQ, HQQ, NF4 and AWQ it is not significant (GPTQ −0.0022).
    - So it is an RTN effect carried by the channel.
- **Implication:**
  - Quantization is reported as a measurement, not a headline.
  - It is out of the abstract (decided 2026-10-12).
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The size-matched unstructured side-module control is deferred (author, 2026-10-11).
  - **Open anomaly:** on T7, the free table under AWQ is damaged on every token (+0.26 to +0.36 nats, against +0.10 for the others), in all 3 seeds. A CPU check (seed 1, full precision, 8 calibration windows, 24 evaluation windows) does *not* reproduce it: AWQ costs C2 +0.109 and C0′ +0.107, and switching C2's channel off during calibration changes nothing (+0.111). So C2 is not fragile under AWQ in general. A GPU re-run (workplace-9a, 12 Oct) narrowed it down: with the default 64 calibration windows the damage reproduces (+0.288), with 8 windows it is normal (+0.103, like C0′'s +0.096). Something in calibration windows 9–64 drives a bad AWQ scale on C2 only, and bf16 is not the cause. A per-layer diagnostic is running. Only the C5 vs C2 AWQ comparison on T7 is affected.
- **Fair?** Yes: the same quantizer and group size for every model, paired bootstraps, and absolute losses reported. One caveat: the earlier documents stated the wrong group size (128); it is 64 on SmolLM2 (corrected 2026-10-11).

### E9-10. Editing the ontology without retraining works a little, on invented words only

- **TL;DR:** after training, writing an ontology entry for a brand-new word improves simple property questions a little, and changing one relation of a known word shifts predictions. Neither works on real text.
- **Experiment:**
  - 300 invented new words and 200 one-relation edits per track, added to the ontology after training, with no weight update.
  - T5 (3 seeds, both hosts; Qwen3 seed 1–2), T4 (3 seeds), T7 (3 seeds).
- **Method:** answer five-way property questions (chance 0.20), entailment and paraphrase items about the new words. For edits, measure how much the model shifts toward the new fact beyond a control edit.
- **Key results:**
  - **T5:**
    - Property selection 0.189 → 0.259 (+0.070*, 360M), and +9–16 points on Qwen3.
    - The gain needs the word's own entry: a random entry gives nothing.
    - Edits on seen words +0.25 log-odds beyond the control edit (360M) and +0.47 (135M).
    - Edits on held-out words work only at 135M.
  - **T4:** nothing moves.
  - **T7:** entailment rises (+0.038*), but a random entry gives the same rise, so it is not the entry's content.
- **Implication:**
  - A proof of concept on synthetic data only.
  - Absolute accuracy stays low (0.26 against chance 0.20).
  - "Editing a non-weight store" is already published; only this mechanism is new.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The standard editing baselines (ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit, in-context editing) on the same items are queued (≈ 27–29 Oct).
  - Larger item sets (v2, about 700 per arm) are queued.
- **Fair?** Within the method, yes (own entry vs random entry vs none). Against other editing methods it is not yet tested, so there is no superiority claim.

### E9-11. Clinical notes (T1c): gain after long terms, none after new or rare ones (seed 1)

- **TL;DR:** on licensed clinical notes the method helps after long multi-token terms (about 1–2%), but not after held-out, unseen or rare terms.
- **Experiment:** T1c: MIMIC-III notes linked to SNOMED CT, SmolLM2-135M and 360M, seed 1. Only aggregate numbers are read; no note text is ever read (decision 58).
- **Method:** the same as E9-1.
- **Key results** (vs C0′):

| | after long terms | inside terms | after held-out | after unseen | after rare |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 360M | −0.93%* | −0.52%* | −0.06% (n.s.) | −0.39% (n.s.) | +0.12% (n.s.) |
| 135M | −1.82%* | −1.22%* | −0.10% (n.s.) | +0.16% (n.s.) | +0.09% (n.s.) |

  - The free table does nothing (C2 − C0′ ≈ 0).
  - The text style is very new to the model (novelty −37 to −41%).
- **Implication:**
  - A third real track shows the same shape as chemistry: a gain after long terms and no zero-shot gain.
  - The ICD-coding task (T1c-F) will say whether this matters downstream.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - Seeds 2–3 and the T1c-ROOD clean held-out test are queued.
  - The T1c section of R9 is not written yet.
- **Fair?** Yes within the track (same recipe), but seed 1 only, and very few unseen and rare targets (924 and 5,274 tokens), so those intervals are wide.

## E4 — small models trained from scratch (core experiment)

### E4-1. Trained from scratch, the method gives a small but real gain and beats a same-size lookup table

- **TL;DR:** in a 50M-parameter model trained from scratch, the composed vectors lower loss a little everywhere and most after rare words (−2.5%), and they beat a free lookup table of the same size.
- **Experiment:**
  - The "operator screen": a GPT-2-style 50M model trained on 100M tokens of web text linked to WordNet, 3 seeds.
  - Conditions: no method (C0), free table (C2), the method (C5), and the method with three other operators.
- **Method:** the same as E9, but the language model learns from scratch together with the composed vectors.
- **Key results** (vs no method / vs free table):

| | all tokens | after held-out terms | after rare terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| C5 − C0 | −0.44%* | −0.37%* | −2.47%* |
| C5 − C2 | −0.14%* | −0.18%* | −1.69%* |

  - After unseen terms: −2.1% (n.s., only 48 target tokens).
  - The data multiplier is 1.024: the method saves about 2% of training data.
  - Training is 2.1% slower.
- **Implication:** the from-scratch signal is real but small, too small to justify scaling up by the pre-registered rule.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - This was a reduced screen. The decisive test is 125M parameters × 500M tokens, with the random-span-vector control C1 (`[[R3]]`).
  - The scaling screen (20M / 50M / 125M × 500M tokens, decision 65) started on 11 Oct; its report is due ≈ 13–14 Oct.
- **Fair?** Yes: 3 seeds, the same evaluation windows, and C2 matched in parameters (2.24M each). Small scale, so exploratory.

### E4-2. Trained from scratch, all binding operators tie

- **TL;DR:** a fixed random operator, translation or no binding does as well as learned HRR when training from scratch.
- **Experiment:** in the same runs as E4-1: C5 against C5rf (fixed random), C5tr (translation) and C5ut (no binding).
- **Key results** (after held-out terms, vs the free table): C5 −0.18%, random −0.25%, translation −0.13%, untyped −0.21% (all *). On all tokens the random operator is numerically best (−0.20%).
- **Implication:** the pre-registered reading is met: the wording is "compositional parameter sharing" in every regime.
- **Gap / fix:** the report should also print the pre-registered paired C5 − arm intervals.
- **Fair?** Yes: the same seeds and windows, with parameters within about 4,000.

## E0 and E2 — mechanism checks (synthetic data and frozen GPT-2)

### E0-1. On planted synthetic data, each part of the mechanism does what it should

- **TL;DR:** in a synthetic world built to match the mechanism, the parts behave as designed:
  - the context query recovers context-dependent meanings;
  - ontology-derived weights transfer to new concepts;
  - the split test finds planted double meanings.
- **Experiment:** CPU experiments, 3 seeds, with planted "teacher" structure. Tests are on held-out concepts and contexts.
- **Key results:**
  - **Context query vs the fixed bundle:** +0.36 to +0.46 cosine. With no context present it does no harm (−0.00001).
  - **Ontology-derived weighting on unseen concepts:** 0.976, against 0.60–0.70 for free per-concept weights.
  - **Split test:**
    - It finds every planted double meaning, but over-splits 37–40% before splits are merged back.
    - After a later fix (fallback for unseen usages), precision is 0.96–1.0, with 0.8% false splits.
- **Implication:** the mechanism *can* represent what it claims to.
- **Gap / fix:** synthetic only. The real-data versions failed (E2-3, E2-4).
- **Fair?** Fair within synthetic data, but the world is built to match the mechanism, so this is an upper bound. The split-test fix was chosen after seeing a development run, then re-run on its own.

### E2-1. Fitting composed vectors to a frozen GPT-2 works badly, and binding does not matter

- **TL;DR:** when composed vectors must match a frozen GPT-2's internal states for WordNet and MeSH concepts:
  - the fit is poor overall;
  - the method's weighting is no better than plain bundling;
  - no binding operator beats a random one.
- **Experiment:**
  - 6,000 WordNet synsets and 6,000 MeSH descriptors.
  - 7 weightings × 7 operators, 3 seeds, test concepts disjoint from training.
- **Key results:**
  - **Ranking quality (MRR):** 0.03–0.04 on WordNet and 0.07–0.08 on MeSH. Variance explained is negative in every cell.
  - **Ontology weighting vs plain bundle:** +0.006 / +0.002 (n.s.).
  - **Best operator vs random fixed:** −0.0001 / −0.0015 (n.s.).
  - **True vs shuffled relation labels:** small but positive, up to +0.014*.
- **Implication:** relation labels carry a little signal, but *how* edges are bound does not matter. This is why the project moved to joint training.
- **Fair?** Yes (cells chosen on validation only), but low absolute scores limit power.

### E2-2. Inserting HRR rows into a frozen model is refuted (F.1)

- **TL;DR:** the project's first idea (fit HRR compositions to a frozen model's word rows) does no better than simpler operators. An earlier apparent lead was a data-leakage artefact.
- **Key results:**
  - additive 0.139 > HRR 0.107;
  - HRR 0.228 vs additive 0.225 (n.s.);
  - diagonal 0.360 > HRR 0.324;
  - the old "best" result fell from 0.439 to 0.338 once the leak was fixed.
- **Implication:** motivation for joint training, not a claim.
- **Fair?** Yes after the audit; the original numbers were not.

### E2-3. A context query helps, but does not pick out word senses (E1, gate failed)

- **TL;DR:** on real sentences (SemCor, frozen GPT-2), the context query improves the fit, but it chooses the right word sense less often than "always guess the most common sense".
- **Key results** (3 seeds):
  - Variance explained +0.66 over the bundle; context MRR +0.051.
  - Sense accuracy is **−0.24** below the most-frequent-sense baseline.
- **Implication:** no sense-alignment claim.
- **Gap:** the draft still marks this section "pending" and should be filled in.
- **Fair?** Yes; reproduced in a clean re-run.

### E2-4. The split test fails on real WordNet (E3, gate failed)

- **TL;DR:** the sense-splitting test that worked on synthetic data does not recover real WordNet senses.
- **Key results:**
  - precision 0.066, recall 0.29;
  - 18.5% false splits (the gate required ≤ 5%);
  - no gain over simply enlarging the model.
- **Implication:** this mechanism (M3) was dropped from the core experiment.
- **Gap:** the draft still marks it "pending".
- **Fair?** Yes; baselines matched on parameters, 3 seeds.

## E10 — a self-learning ontology

### E10-1. A learnable ontology works on planted data, but simple baselines explain most of it

- **TL;DR:** letting the model edit its own ontology recovers erased relations in a synthetic world. But a no-learning readout and standard rule mining (AMIE) do nearly as well, and the acceptance test accepts fake relations in worlds that have none.
- **Experiment:** a synthetic world (304 concepts, 7 relations, 3 seeds), with knowledge-graph-embedding, rule-mining and "null world" controls.
- **Method:** the ontology's edges are learnable, pulled towards the curated version. Each proposed edit is accepted only if it improves held-out fit against a matched control.
- **Key results:**
  - **Recovery of erased edges:** AUC 0.98–0.99; a no-learning readout already scores 0.91–0.92.
  - **Discovered relations:** Jaccard 0.44, against 0.84 for an oracle.
  - **Null worlds:** 2.7–4.3 fake relations accepted per run.
- **Implication:** reported as a methods / negative result, not as a protocol claim.
- **Gap / fix:** an acceptance test calibrated on null worlds; the real-model test (E10.2) has not been run.
- **Fair?** Baselines matched; the world uses the learner's own operator family, so this is an upper bound.

### E10-2. On WordNet with a frozen GPT-2, nothing transfers

- **TL;DR:** on real WordNet the self-learning ontology is no better than standard baselines.
- **Key results:**
  - Recovery 0.589 vs ComplEx 0.628 (n.s.).
  - The self-acceptance test is at chance (0.46 vs 0.45).
  - New-word frames F1 0.007 vs 0.267 for nearest neighbour.
- **Fair?** Yes, but in the frozen-model regime that already failed.

### E10-3. Active self-reflection + passive learning is not faster (E10.9a)

- **TL;DR:** committing tested "explanations" (relation rules) as explicit updates does not make learning faster at the same amount of data, though it does add correct edges for never-observed concepts.
- **Experiment:** a pre-registered synthetic study, 5 seeds, 715 runs. Arms: hybrid (passive + active), passive at matched data, and content, generator and compute controls.
- **Method:** the hybrid periodically tests a hypothesis about a learned relation (e.g. "symmetric") on held-out pairs and, if it passes, writes it into the weights.
- **Key results:**
  - **Speed (primary):** H − P −0.003 [−0.019, +0.013], not faster.
  - **At equal gradient data:** +0.021 [+0.006, +0.035] better fit, all of it from rule-implied edges for unseen concepts.
  - **In a world with no structure:** 0 of 19 wrong commitments were retracted, though they were harmless.
- **Implication:** no "learns faster" claim. The follow-up (E10.9b) is not run because its gate was not met.
- **Fair?** Yes: matched data and compute, pre-registered. A development pilot ran before the pre-registration, which is disclosed.

## Binding programme — does the "VSA" store hold usable structure? (T5, 3 seeds)

### B-1. The relations stored in a word's vector can be read back almost perfectly

- **TL;DR:** algebraically "unbinding" a word's composed vector recovers its related words almost perfectly. A fixed random operator does this as well as a learned one.
- **Experiment:** decoding tests on trained T5 models (360M, 3 seeds; replicated at 135M and on Qwen3, 2 seeds), comparing learned HRR, fixed random binding (C5rf), no binding (C5ut) and translation (C5tr).
- **Method:** apply the inverse of each relation's operator to the word's vector, then look up the nearest known concept.
- **Key results:**
  - **Held-out words:** mean reciprocal rank 0.987, against 0.212 for guessing by frequency (+0.77*).
  - **Learned vs fixed random operator:** −0.006 (n.s.).
  - **A linear probe** on the same vector does worse than the algebra (−0.20).
- **Implication:** the store is readable, and that comes from the algebra, not from training the operator.
- **Fair?** Yes.

### B-2. The model does not use the stored roles by itself (pre-registered P1: null)

- **TL;DR:** given only the vectors, no model can tell apart two new words whose relations are swapped (A *depends on* X and is *part of* Y; B the reverse).
- **Experiment:** 300 such "twin" pairs, 360M (3 seeds), 135M (3 seeds) and Qwen3 (2 seeds).
- **Key results:**
  - All arms score 0.48–0.50, where chance is 0.50.
  - **Binding vs no binding:** −0.004 (n.s.). **Binding vs translation:** +0.002 (n.s.).
- **Pre-registered prediction:** 0.55–0.65 for C5. **Failed.**
- **Implication:** binding is unused, not useless. The roles are there (B-1), but next-token training does not teach the model to read them.
- **Gap / fix:** the trained-readout arms, which train the model to unbind, are queued (≈ 17–19 Oct).
- **Fair?** Yes. On Qwen3 the no-binding and translation arms are missing.

### B-3. Binding beats simpler operators only for reverse lookup with ambiguous roles

- **TL;DR:** chained (two-hop) lookups need one store per word, and binding beats a plain sum only when a related word could fill more than one role.
- **Key results:**
  - **Two-hop chains, per-word stores vs one shared memory:** +0.81*.
  - **Reverse lookup with ambiguous fillers:** binding beats no binding by +0.38* and translation by +0.19*.
  - **Chaining:** binding ties no binding (+0.011, n.s.) and beats translation (+0.11).
  - **Word order along a path:** not encoded by any operator (0.49–0.50), as expected for this kind of binding.
  - **Capacity** (random-vector sweep at 256 dimensions): recovery is 0.98 with 8 relations per word, 0.66 with 16 and 0.24 with 32.
- **Implication:**
  - The VSA framing is justified for explicit lookup with ambiguous roles, not for language modelling.
  - The capacity limit matters for real ontologies with many relations per term (T4 entries reach 24).
- **Gap / fix:** a word-order-aware operator, and the same step on T4/T1/T7/WordNet, are queued.
- **Fair?** Yes.

## E12 — decoding the store into the prompt ("explicit recall")

### E12-1. Decoding the store into text lets the model resolve swapped roles (pre-registered, passed)

- **TL;DR:** if the stored relations are decoded into a few lines of text before the question, the model tells role-swapped twins apart (0.78 against chance 0.50). It works for any host that reads the store.
- **Experiment:** 300 twin pairs, 360M, 3 seeds.
  - **Hosts:** C5, C0′, no-binding C5ut, and P0 (one run).
  - **Prompt text (context):** nothing; text decoded from each store; the gold relations as text; a prose definition.
- **Method:** decode each relation from the word's vector, snap it to the nearest concept, and write lines like "X depends on Y (0.58)" before the question.
- **Key results:**

| Context given to the model | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| Nothing | 0.50 |
| Decoded from its HRR store | **0.78** |
| Decoded from a no-binding store | 0.50 |
| Gold relations as text | 0.86 |
| Prose definition | 0.74 |

  - **Decoded − nothing:** +0.28 [+0.26, +0.30]*.
  - **HRR store − no-binding store:** +0.27*.
  - **Plain fine-tuned model reading the same store:** the same (−0.009, n.s.), so the roles live in the store, not in the model.
- **Implication:** this is the main evidence for keeping "VSA" in the paper, as an explicit, readable store.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The stores are built from gold relations.
  - There is no count of tokens yet. The claim that "the store costs fewer tokens than a definition" is not yet measured.
  - Qwen3 runs are pending.
- **Fair?** Yes, pre-registered. One caveat: by design the twins' no-binding stores are identical, so the HRR-vs-no-binding contrast is close to guaranteed.

### E12-2. The model uses the decoded related words, but not their roles

- **TL;DR:** removing one decoded relation changes the answer as expected (the model follows the text), but binding makes this slightly *weaker* than a no-binding store, and roles add nothing.
- **Key results:**
  - **Faithfulness:** +0.36*.
  - **Binding − no binding:** −0.044* (predicted ≈ 0; failed in the negative direction).
  - **Role specificity:** −0.044.
- **Implication:** roles matter to behaviour only through the decoded text.
- **Fair?** Yes.

### E12-3. The same on real chemistry items (T4, seed 1)

- **TL;DR:** on chemistry items, the decoded store (0.88) does about as well as the gold relations (0.89) and slightly worse than a definition (0.91).
- **Key results:**
  - Accuracy: nothing 0.55, decoded 0.88, no-binding store 0.50, translation store 0.51, gold 0.89, definition 0.91.
  - A plain fine-tuned host reading the store: 0.88.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - Single seed and no intervals; seeds 2–3 are queued (≈ 16 Oct).
  - The "definition" here is generated from the gold relations, not real ChEBI text.
- **Fair?** Descriptive only so far.

### E12-4. Chained recall works; reverse-lookup text barely helps

- **TL;DR:** decoding a two-step chain lets the model answer two-hop questions, but decoded reverse lookups barely help.
- **Key results:**
  - **Two-hop:** T5 0.19 → 0.80 (gold 0.84); T4 0.23 → 0.62.
  - **Reverse lookup:** T5 0.52 → 0.54; T4 0.55 → 0.48 (worse).
  - The untouched model (P0, one run) reads in-context text best (definition 0.96).
- **Implication:** the strong algebraic reverse lookup (B-3) does not carry over into behaviour.
- **Fair?** No intervals yet.

## E11 — learning a new word by reading its definition once (new since 8 Oct)

*Note: in E11 loss rows a **positive** number means the loss went **up**, which is worse.*

### E11-1. On invented words, reading a definition once helps a little; on real chemistry it does not (P1 passed, P2 failed)

- **TL;DR:** after reading a prose definition once, without any weight change, the method answers questions about a new invented word about 3 points better. On real chemistry definitions it does not help, and even the gold relations slightly hurt.
- **Experiment:**
  - **T5:** 300 invented words with prose definitions (360M C5, 3 seeds).
  - **T4:** 341 held-out ChEBI terms with their real definitions (3 seeds).
  - **Conditions:** the frame read from the definition ("linker"), no frame, the gold frame, random frames, and a "type prior" frame.
- **Method:** a fixed matcher finds known concepts in the definition. The model picks each one's relation by which choice makes the definition most likely, and the resulting frame is written into the ontology.
- **Key results:**
  - **T5 property questions:**
    - read frame − none: +0.031 [+0.025, +0.038]*;
    - gold frame − none: +0.070;
    - read frame − random: +0.016*.
  - **T5, the model's own relation choices:** worse than a simple type-frequency table (−0.038*).
  - **T4, loss after the term:**
    - read frame − none: +0.13% (worse);
    - read frame − random-relation frame: +0.00% (p 0.95);
    - gold frame − none: +0.18% (worse).
  - On T5 the read frame recovers about 44% of the gold frame's gain (derived: 0.031 / 0.070). The pre-registered "useful" bar was 50%.
- **Pre-registered:** P1 (T5) passed. P2 (natural text) failed. The "self-reflective" reading fails, because the model's own relation choice is worse than a frequency table.
- **Implication:** one-shot learning from text works on synthetic data only, and recovers under half of the gold gain. Drop the "self-reflective" wording.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The recovery share, relation accuracy and frame precision/recall are not printed in the report.
  - The 135M, C2 and P0 runs and the other sets were not run.
  - The T7-ROOD E11 jobs are queued (≈ 22 Oct).
- **Fair?** Yes: pre-registered (amendment written before any run), 3 seeds, paired items.

### E11-2. Text routes beat frames for single words; frames win only on "zero tokens per use"

- **TL;DR:** putting the definition in the prompt, or taking a few gradient steps on it, beats writing a frame by a wide margin on invented words. The frame's only advantage is that it costs no prompt tokens afterwards and changes no weights.
- **Key results:**
  - **T5 property, definition in prompt vs read frame:** +0.42*.
  - **T5 property, compute-matched gradient steps vs read frame:** +0.27*.
  - **Frame on top of the in-context definition:** +0.008* (a small extra gain).
  - **T4 loss:** the in-context definition +0.74% (worse); gradient steps +79% (much worse; needs a sanity check).
  - **Cost:** the in-context definition uses 109 tokens per query; the frame uses 0 per query but 1,816 tokens of computation to write.
- **Implication:** the frame route is a persistence mechanism (zero tokens per use), not a more accurate one.
- **Gap / fix:**
  - The gradient route's damage to general text is not reported.
  - The +79% needs checking.
- **Fair?** Compute is matched by design; the same items.

### E11-3. A dense encoding of the definition does worse than the read frame

- **TL;DR:** encoding the definition into one vector (the C6d style) answers fewer questions correctly than the read frame. The two tie on loss.
- **Key results:**
  - **Property questions, encoder − read frame:** T5 −0.023*, T4 −0.015*.
  - **T4 loss:** −0.03% (n.s.).
  - **Cost to write:** the encoder is about 25–50× cheaper (73 against 1,816 tokens).
- **Fair?** Partly: the encoder's projector was trained on verbalized frames, not natural definitions.

### E11-4. With many new words, the frame gain holds while the in-context gain fades

- **TL;DR:** as the number of new words grows (25 to 400), the frame's gain stays constant, while putting all definitions in the prompt loses value. At 200 words the read frames tie in-context reading and the gold frames win. Retrieving the one right definition beats everything.
- **Key results** (T5, 3 seeds):
  - **Frame − none:** +0.035 to +0.042 at every size.
  - **In-context − none:** falls from +0.36 to +0.03.
  - **At 200 words:**
    - read frame − in-context: −0.010 (n.s.; null, as predicted);
    - gold frame − in-context: +0.033*.
  - **Retrieval (one definition) − read frame:** +0.45 to +0.51.
  - **T4:** frames ≈ none.
- **Implication:** the defensible claim is narrow: frames are a zero-token way to keep many new words available. Retrieval is the strongest route.
- **Gap / fix:** the report lacks the forgetting, interference and cost numbers, and only one word order was run.
- **Fair?** Mostly. Retrieval queries with the word's own prompt, which favours it.
