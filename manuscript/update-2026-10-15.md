# VSA-LLM results update (15 October 2026)

What we learned between 12 and 15 October, in plain language. It follows [update-2026-10-12.md](update-2026-10-12.md), which covers everything up to 12 October.

**These numbers are preliminary.** They come straight from the generated reports and have not yet been checked into R9 or the claims ledger.

## The short version

1. **Putting a term's definition in the prompt does not replace the method. The two add up.** This is the strongest new result in the method's favour.
2. **On real text, a big enough per-term lookup table does as well as the composed vectors after long terms.** "Sharing building blocks" is therefore not the right explanation of the gain on natural text, although composition still wins clearly for unseen and rare terms.
3. **Our attempted fix for the "held-out cost" (link dropout) did not work.**
4. **Smaller models benefit more**, confirmed at three seeds.
5. **Clinical notes confirm the earlier pattern:** a gain after long terms, nothing for new words.
6. **Training small models from scratch at three sizes:** a steady but small gain that does not grow with size. By our pre-registered rule we do not scale up further.

## How to read this

- **The method** ("C5") adds a vector to the language model at the end of each multi-token term (for example a chemical name). That vector is built from the term's entry in an ontology, such as "is a kind of …" or "contains element …".
- **The baseline** ("C0′") is the same model fine-tuned on the same text without the method. Unless stated, every percentage is the change in the model's prediction error (loss) on the text that follows a term, compared with this baseline. **Negative means better.**
- **Kinds of term:**
  - **Held-out:** deliberately kept out of the method's training. Their vectors are built from the ontology alone at test time.
  - **Unseen:** listed in the ontology but absent from the training text.
  - **Rare:** seen 1–9 times.
  - **Long:** three or more sub-word pieces.
- **Tracks** (test domains):
  - T5: invented company glossary (synthetic).
  - T4: real chemistry text.
  - T7: 2025–26 medical abstracts with 2026 MeSH names.
  - T1c: clinical notes.
- **Statistics:** `*` means the difference is statistically reliable after correcting for multiple comparisons. "3 seeds" means three independent training runs.

## 1. Does a definition in the prompt make the method unnecessary?

**The experiment.**
- Before each piece of test text, we pasted a short glossary: one line per term in that text, giving the term's name and its ontology entry written out as sentences (for example "X: It is a type of … It contains element …").
- The model then predicted the text as usual.
- We compared four set-ups: no glossary, glossary only, method only, and method plus glossary.
- As a control, we also used a glossary describing *other* terms, to separate real information from simply having more text in front.
- Tracks: T5, T4 and T7, on SmolLM2-360M with 3 seeds.

**The problem it addresses.** The obvious objection to the method is: "why not just put the definition in the prompt?" Our novelty review listed this as a required baseline, and it had not been run.

**What we found:**

| Track and terms | Glossary alone | Method alone | Method, on top of the glossary |
|---|---|---|---|
| T5 synthetic, held-out terms | −12.0%* | −13.3%* | a further −10.8%* (−21.5% together) |
| T4 chemistry, unseen terms | −0.8%* | −2.3%* | a further −2.2%* |
| T4 chemistry, rare terms | −0.2% | −0.9%* | a further −0.8%* |
| T4 chemistry, long terms | −0.5%* | −1.1%* | a further −1.0%* |
| T4 chemistry, held-out terms | −0.7%* | −0.4%* | a further −0.4%* |
| T7 medical, long terms | +0.1%* (slightly worse) | −0.6%* | a further −0.5%* |

- The glossary's information is real: the right glossary beats a glossary about other terms on every track.
- On T7 the glossary does not help the fine-tuned model at all; after long terms it is even slightly worse (+0.1%*).
- An untouched pretrained model gains more from the glossary than the fine-tuned model does (T4: −2.3% vs −0.7% after held-out terms).

**What it means.**
- The method is not a stand-in for a definition in the prompt. It adds information on top of one, on every track and term type we tested.
- On real chemistry text the method beats the definition for unseen, rare and long terms. The definition is better only for held-out terms.
- The pre-registration predicted the opposite (the method would add little once the definition was present), so this prediction failed in the method's favour.
- The method also costs no prompt space at all, while the glossary used up to 1,024 tokens per window on T4 and T5.

**Was it fair?** Mostly. Two caveats:
- **The glossary was capped at 1,024 tokens.** On T4 and T5 it reached that cap, so it covered only 45% (T4) and 79% (T5) of the terms in each window. The glossary is therefore somewhat handicapped there. On T7 it covered 100%.
- **The definitions are written from the ontology entry,** not taken from real dictionary or textbook definitions, so they hold the same information the method reads.

**Gaps and next steps.**
- Re-run T4 and T5 with a larger cap (the models accept 8,192 tokens) so every term is covered. This is evaluation only and cheap.
- The same comparison on T7-ROOD (the clean held-out test) is queued for about Oct 19–20.

## 2. Is the gain "shared building blocks", or just a big enough per-term vector?

**The experiment.**
- We added a free lookup table that gives every term its own learned vector at the composed vector's full width ("C2w"): 256 numbers instead of 34 on T4.
- Earlier we had seen that vectors composed from the *wrong* relations still beat a narrow free table on chemistry. That led us to explain the gain as "sharing building blocks across terms".
- C2w tests that explanation.
- Tracks: T4 and T7, 360M, 3 seeds.

**The problem it addresses.** The narrow table (34 numbers per term) might simply have been too small, which would make "sharing" the wrong explanation.

**What we found** (vs the baseline):

| | Method (correct relations) | Wrong relations | Wide free table (C2w) | Narrow free table (C2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T4, after long terms | −1.09% | −0.89% | **−0.95%** | −0.23% |
| T4, after unseen terms | −2.29% | −1.48% | −1.08% | — |
| T4, after rare terms | −0.91% | −0.54% | −0.37% | — |
| T4, after held-out terms | −0.40% | −0.41% | −0.61% | — |
| T7, after long terms | −0.57% | −0.56% | −0.60% | −0.46% |

- **Parameters:** the wide table uses 17.3M parameters on T4, against 2.35M for the method (7.5× more). On T7 it uses 2.1M against 1.1M.

**What it means.**
- After long terms, a wide enough free table matches the wrong-relations code. By the rule we wrote down before running, the explanation "sharing building blocks beats a free table" is withdrawn for natural text. The honest wording is "a per-term vector of sufficient size".
- Two things still favour composition:
  - **Efficiency:** it reaches the same result with about 7× fewer parameters.
  - **New and rare terms:** it clearly beats the wide table where a free table has little or nothing to learn from (unseen −2.3% vs −1.1%; rare −0.9% vs −0.4%).
- The wide table does better after held-out terms (−0.61%). This fits the earlier finding that injecting a composed vector for words the model already learned from text slightly hurts.

**Was it fair?** By design the comparison is *not* parameter-matched: the wide table gets far more parameters. It answers "is it the vector's width?", not "is it the parameter budget?".

**Gaps and next steps.** The clean test is a wide table built from a *small shared basis*, with the same total parameters as the method. That separates width from sharing at equal budget. It is not yet queued (see "Proposed experiments").

## 3. Can a training trick remove the "held-out cost"? (link dropout)

**The experiment.** During training we left out the injected vector at random for 30% of the term mentions ("C5ld"). Track: T7 (2026 medical names), 360M, 3 seeds.

**The problem it addresses.** On T7 the method *hurts* slightly after held-out names (+0.48%). Those names appear in the training text without the method, so the model learned them as plain words. At test time it suddenly receives a vector it never saw for them. The idea was that if the model also sees known words *without* their vector during training, an unexpected vector should disturb it less.

**What we found:**
- **After held-out names:** +0.46% with link dropout, against +0.48% without (no real difference).
- **After long and rare terms:** link dropout costs a little (−0.46% vs −0.57% after long terms).
- **Inside the trained model:** link dropout made the model rely less on each term's vector (removing them costs +0.61% instead of +0.97% after long terms). But removing the vector after held-out names still *helps* by the same amount as before (−0.44%).

**What it means.**
- The pre-registered prediction failed. The held-out cost is not caused by the model always seeing known words with their vectors during training.
- A simpler deployment rule remains: **only add vectors for terms the model has not already learned from text.**

**Was it fair?** Yes: the same recipe and data, only the dropout changed, with 3 seeds. Only one dropout rate (30%) was tried.

**Gaps and next steps.** Link dropout is dropped. The clean test of new-word benefit is T7-ROOD, whose training text has the held-out names removed (≈ Oct 19–20).

## 4. Do smaller models benefit more? (chemistry at 135M, 3 seeds)

**The experiment.** The chemistry track (T4) repeated on the smaller SmolLM2-135M with seeds 2 and 3 added (seed 1 already existed). The comparison is with 360M at 3 seeds.

**The problem it addresses.** At one seed, the smaller model seemed to gain more. That needed confirming before it could be claimed.

**What we found:**

| T4 chemistry | 135M (3 seeds) | 360M (3 seeds) |
|---|---|---|
| after unseen terms | **−3.62%*** | −2.29%* |
| after long terms | **−1.72%*** | −1.09%* |
| after rare terms | **−1.58%*** | −0.91%* |
| inside terms | −1.98%* | −1.54%* |
| after held-out terms | −0.54%* | −0.40%* |

The intervals of the two sizes do not overlap for unseen, long, rare and inside terms; for held-out terms they overlap.

**What it means.** On real text, the smaller model gains more, as the seed-1 result suggested. That fits the idea that the method helps most where the model's own capacity to learn a new term is weakest.

**Was it fair?** Yes: the same recipe, data, test set and seeds.

**Gaps and next steps.** Only two sizes were compared. The from-scratch scaling screen (section 7) gives a broader picture.

## 5. New 2026 medical names on the smaller model (T7 at 135M, seed 1)

**The experiment.** The T7 track (PubMed 2025–26 abstracts with 2026 MeSH names) on SmolLM2-135M, one seed.

**The problem it addresses.** It tests whether the size effect from section 4 holds on a second real domain, and whether T7's held-out cost depends on model size.

**What we found:**

| T7 | 135M (seed 1) | 360M (3 seeds) |
|---|---|---|
| after rare terms | −1.04%* | −0.50%* |
| after long terms | −1.09%* | −0.57%* |
| inside terms | −0.52%* | −0.28%* |
| after held-out terms | +0.01% (no cost) | **+0.48%*** (a cost) |

**What it means.**
- The gain is about twice as large on the smaller model.
- The held-out cost disappears at 135M.

**Was it fair?** Yes within the track, but this is one seed.

**Gaps and next steps.** Seeds 2–3 at 135M are not queued; they would cost about 2 GPU-hours plus evaluations (see "Proposed experiments").

## 6. Clinical notes at three seeds (T1c)

**The experiment.**
- Clinical notes (MIMIC-III, licensed) linked to the SNOMED CT ontology, on SmolLM2-135M and 360M, with seeds 2–3 added to seed 1.
- Only aggregate numbers are read; no note text is ever viewed.

**The problem it addresses.** It is a third real domain, and the closest to a medical application.

**What we found:**

| T1c clinical (3 seeds) | 360M | 135M |
|---|---|---|
| after long terms | −0.94%* | −1.84%* |
| inside terms | −0.53%* | −1.23%* |
| after unseen terms | −0.48%* (now significant) | +0.02% |
| after held-out terms | −0.02% | −0.13% |
| after rare terms | −0.02% | +0.04% |
| text without terms | −0.06%* | −0.10%* |

**What it means.**
- The seed-1 picture holds: a reliable gain after and inside long clinical terms, larger on the smaller model.
- There is no gain for held-out or rare terms. At 360M there is a small gain for unseen ones.

**Was it fair?** Yes: the same recipe, 3 seeds. The unseen and rare groups are small (about 900 and 5,000 tokens), so their intervals are wide.

**Gaps and next steps.**
- The clinical coding task (assigning rare ICD diagnosis codes, T1c-F) is training now; results ≈ Oct 16.
- The clean held-out test (T1c-ROOD) is ≈ Oct 25.

## 7. Training small models from scratch at three sizes (scaling screen)

**The experiment.**
- Small GPT-style language models trained from scratch at three sizes: about 20M, 50M and 125M parameters.
- Each was trained on 500M tokens of general web text whose WordNet multi-word terms are linked to the ontology. Seed 1 only (an exploratory screen by design).
- Compared:
  - no method;
  - a free lookup table;
  - the method;
  - two "hybrids" (method plus a free per-term vector, as in the HRRBERT paper we build on);
  - shuffled relations (50M);
  - a variant with a growing dictionary (50M, "C5dev").

**The problem it addresses.** Whether the method's benefit grows, holds or fades as models get bigger. This decides whether larger from-scratch runs are worth it.

**What we found:**

| From scratch (seed 1) | 20M | 50M | 125M |
|---|---|---|---|
| method, all text | −0.24%* | −0.38%* | −0.29%* |
| method, after long terms | −1.20%* | −1.40%* | −1.13%* |
| method, after held-out terms | +0.02% | −0.05% | +0.25% |
| free table, all text | −0.30%* | −0.27%* | −0.19%* |
| hybrid (HRRAdd), all text | −0.36%* | −0.49%* | −0.35%* |
| extra data the baseline needs, all text | 1.7% | 4.5% | 3.1% |
| extra data the baseline needs, after long terms | 14% | 15% | 12% |

"Extra data the baseline needs" is how much more training data the model without the method needs to reach the same loss as the model with it.

- **Hybrids:** best at every size, but their edge over the method shrinks from −0.13% (20M) to −0.06% (125M).
- **Shuffled relations (50M):** the method beats them (−0.16% on all text, −0.50% after long terms, −0.36% after held-out terms). Correct relations matter when training from scratch, unlike in the fine-tuned models.
- **Free table's share of the gain:** at 20M the free table does as well as the method or better; at 50M and 125M it gets about 70% of the method's gain.
- **Growing dictionary (C5dev):** about the same as the method.
- **Pre-registered decision:** **do not scale up** (no "phase 2"). The benefit is real but does not grow from 50M to 125M.

**What it means.**
- From scratch, the method gives a small, steady benefit: about 0.3% overall and over 1% after long terms. Without the method, a model needs about 2–5% more training data overall to match it, and 12–15% more for the text after long terms.
- That benefit does not grow with model size, so on this evidence it is not a "scales up" story.
- New words composed zero-shot do not benefit here either.
- The hybrid design is consistently a little better than the composed vector alone.

**Was it fair?** As a screen, yes: the same data, recipe and evaluation for every arm, and the free table matched in parameters. But it is one seed per size. The intervals cover only evaluation noise; seed variance is added from an earlier three-seed screen.

**Gaps and next steps.**
- More seeds or a 350M model are *not* requested by the pre-registered rule; that would be your call.
- Testing the hybrid design in pretrained models (sections 1–6) is a natural follow-up (see "Proposed experiments").

## Idea under evaluation: does a richer ontology help more? (proposed, 15 Oct)

**The idea** (author, 15 Oct). Instead of changing the model's size, keep the model fixed and change the ontology's *size and granularity*. If the method really draws on the ontology's knowledge, a richer, more detailed ontology should help more than a thin one. A larger model might also exploit a rich ontology better than a small one. The toolkit could then be used to *build* a richer ontology that the model uses itself.

**Why this is a better test than model size alone.** It goes straight at the paper's central question: does the ontology's content cause the gain? Our current evidence cannot answer that cleanly:
- Across tracks the gain follows how new the words are, but newness and ontology richness move together.
- Model-size runs keep the ontology fixed, so they say nothing about the ontology.
- A dose–response in ontology richness at a fixed model and fixed text isolates the ontology's contribution. If more (true) relations give more benefit, and shuffled relations do not, the content is causal.

**What we already know that bears on it.**

1. **The ontologies differ in richness far more than we had noted**, and in the same order as the content effect.

   | Track | Relation types | Edges per term | What the edges mostly say | Does the content matter? (correct vs shuffled relations) |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | T5 synthetic | 23 | 7.4 | meaningful facts (purpose, owner, area, dependencies) | a lot: shuffling destroys the gain |
   | T4 chemistry | 13 | 8.1 | composition and chemistry (elements 32%, is-a 24%, roles 10%) | a little: −0.20% after long terms |
   | T7 2026 MeSH | 7 | 5.2 | mostly position in the MeSH tree (75% tree or record-class codes, 4% drug action) | not at all |
   | T1 MeSH | 5 | 4.4 | tree codes (58%) and parent (31%) | not at all (no gain) |

   This fits the idea, but it is confounded with how new the words are.
2. **From scratch, the correct relations beat shuffled ones** (−0.5% after long terms at 50M), on WordNet's fairly rich frames.
3. **Capacity limit.** One 256-number vector can hold about 8 relations almost perfectly, 16 only partly (66% recovered) and 32 poorly (24%), in our capacity sweep. A much richer ontology may need wider vectors, or the extra relations will blur together.
4. **A model writing its own ontology works poorly so far.** In E11 a model reading a definition recovered under half of the gold frame's benefit on invented words and none on chemistry. In E10, self-edited ontologies accepted false relations.
5. **A stronger LLM can write frames cheaply and fairly accurately.** The toolkit's "teacher" pilot had Claude write relations from WordNet glosses: 400 entries for $2.41, agreeing with WordNet at precision 0.78 and recall 0.75. Its from-scratch arm (C5teach) is designed and pre-registered, but waiting for approval.

**Verdict: worth running, in stages.**
- It is the most direct test available of whether the ontology's knowledge matters.
- It has a practical answer: whether richer ontologies are worth building.
- Most of it needs no new data.
- Building the richer ontology with a *teacher* model is feasible now. A model building its *own* is the weakest link and should come last.

**Proposed design.** One fixed text, test set and recipe. Only the ontology frames change.

- **Stage 1 — make an existing ontology thinner** (no new data; T4 chemistry; SmolLM2-135M and 360M; 3 seeds):
  - **Levels:**
    1. "is-a" only;
    2. is-a plus composition (elements, charge);
    3. the full ontology (existing runs);
    4. half of each term's edges, chosen at random.
  - **Controls:** the existing shuffled-relations runs. The richest level's composed vector is also widened to 512 (capacity check).
  - **What it shows:** whether the gain grows with richness, whether the larger model gains more from the extra detail (your interaction hypothesis), and whether vector width limits it.
  - **Cost:** about 18 new runs, roughly 20 GPU-hours plus evaluations. A small code addition is needed (keep only chosen relation types or a fraction of edges).
- **Stage 2 — make a thin ontology richer** (T7-ROOD, whose held-out names are absent from training, so it is the clean new-word test; 135M and 360M; 3 seeds). Two ways to enrich:
  - **(a) Using the ontology itself, free:** add each term's inherited facts. For example, give a new drug its mapped MeSH heading's drug actions and parent concepts (two-hop expansion).
  - **(b) With a teacher:** Claude reads each 2026 MeSH record's note and writes richer relations (class, mechanism, target, use), constrained to the existing dictionary. MeSH is public data, so nothing licensed is sent. About 8,200 entries ≈ **$50**, which needs your approval.
  - **Controls:** the enriched frames shuffled between terms (amount vs content), and the same enriched definitions given *in the prompt* instead (does the method still add on top?).
  - **Cost:** about 12 runs, roughly 15 GPU-hours plus evaluations.
- **Stage 3 — the model enriches its own ontology** (only if stage 2 shows richness helps): the toolkit's read–write loop (E11 reader, E13 cycle) writes the extra relations itself. The comparison is against the teacher-built version.
- **Optionally,** one level pair on Qwen3-1.7B (a larger, modern host, about 4 GPU-hours per run) to test "larger models use richer ontologies better" beyond the SmolLM2 sizes.

**What would count as a yes** (to be pre-registered before any run):
1. The gain rises with richness at a fixed model.
2. Shuffled frames stay flat at every level.
3. The enriched ontology beats the base one on T7-ROOD after held-out names.
4. The interaction question: the larger model's gain grows faster with richness than the smaller model's (your hypothesis). Current evidence points the other way at a *fixed* ontology (smaller models gain more), so this is a genuine open question.

If the gain is flat beyond the thinnest level and shuffled frames match, the method is a structured-capacity trick, not a knowledge channel. That would also be worth knowing.

**Caveats on fairness.**
- **"Richer" mixes several things:** more edges per term, more relation types, more correct relations, and possibly more linked terms. The design varies them one at a time and keeps the linked terms and text fixed.
- **Teacher frames bring in a bigger model's knowledge.** A gain could be distillation from Claude rather than richness as such. The shuffled-teacher and in-prompt controls separate the two.
- **Capacity:** without the wider-vector check, "richer does not help" could just mean "the vector is full".
- **Parameter counts** change slightly with more relations and atoms, and will be reported.

**Recommendation.**
- Run **stage 1 now**: cheap, no new data or spending, about 20 GPU-hours.
- Prepare stage 2(a) in parallel.
- Ask you about the $50 teacher spend for stage 2(b) once stage 1 shows whether richness matters at all.
- Stage 1 can be ready to queue (code, tests, pre-registration) in about half a day of work.

## What it adds up to

- **The best case for the method is now:** it adds information beyond a definition in the prompt, costs no prompt space, and helps most for genuinely new or rare terms and for smaller models.
- **The mechanism is not "sharing building blocks"** on real text. A wide enough per-term table matches it after long terms, though it needs 7× more parameters and does worse on new and rare terms.
- **Zero-shot vectors for held-out terms still do not help on real text** when those terms already appear in the training text. The clean test (T7-ROOD) is days away.
- **Training from scratch:** a small, steady gain that does not grow with size.
