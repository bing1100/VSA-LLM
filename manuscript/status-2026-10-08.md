# VSA-LLM: where we stand (8 October 2026)

A plain-language status of the program: what we tested, what we found, what it means, and what is still running.
Numbers are from committed reports (sources at the end). Unless marked otherwise they are means over 3 training seeds
with 95% intervals. Results marked **pilot** are single-seed checks that are not pre-registered endpoints.

---

## 1. Bottom line

1. **The method works when a language model meets words it has never seen.** Adding a vector built from a word's
   ontology entry (its relations, e.g. *is-a*, *treats*, *part-of*) lowers the model's prediction error on the text
   that follows the word. On invented words the reduction is 9–20%.
2. **It barely helps on words the model already knows, and that is most real text tested so far.** On real chemistry
   text the gain is 0.4–4%. On clinical (MeSH/PubMed) text and on WordNet it is zero. The size of the gain tracks how
   new the vocabulary is to the model.
3. **The gain comes from composing a word's vector out of its relations, not from the "VSA" binding math.** Replacing
   the circular-convolution binding with simpler operators gives the same or better loss. Scrambling which relations
   belong to which word destroys the gain.
4. **The binding does store usable structure, but the model does not use it unless asked to decode it.** The stored
   relations can be read back almost perfectly with algebra. The model itself, though, cannot tell apart two new
   words whose relations are swapped. When the stored relations are decoded into the prompt as text, it can: from
   chance to 78% correct (3 seeds, pre-registered), and from 55% to 88% on real chemistry text (seed 1). This is the one place where the VSA binding beats the simpler operators, and only
   when a related word could fill more than one role. For decoding and store queries a fixed random binding works as
   well as a learned one.
5. **The "self-learning" extension did not deliver.** Pairing active self-reflection with passive learning was not
   faster than passive learning alone.
6. **T5 alone will not carry a paper** (section 2). The decisive real-world results are due between about 10 and
   12 October: chemistry at 3 seeds, a new-drug track built from 2025–26 PubMed, and the clinical tracks.

## 2. Is T5 enough for a manuscript? No.

**What T5 is.** A synthetic enterprise glossary: invented words with invented definitions, written into templated
text. It was built this way on purpose. No pretrained model can have seen these words, so the test is free of
contamination and shows the largest effect the method can have.

**Why it is not enough on its own.**
- Reviewers will read T5 as a toy. Its words are new by construction and its text is generated from the same
  ontology the method reads, so the setup favours the method.
- The real-text results so far are small (chemistry, T4: −0.4% after held-out terms, −2.3% after unseen terms at
  seed 1) or null (clinical T1-open, WordNet). On its own, T5 would overstate what a user gets.
- Two of the three headline claims hold only on T5. The ontology-editing effect (claim C) is null on T4. Under 4-bit
  quantization the T4 gain partly disappears.

**What T5 is still good for.** It is the controlled upper bound, and the place to run mechanism controls cheaply
(shuffled frames, operator swaps, copy analysis). A paper can keep it in that role.

**What would make the paper convincing.**

| Need | Status |
|---|---|
| A real text domain whose vocabulary is genuinely new to the model, with a clear gain at 3 seeds | **T7** is that test: 2025–26 PubMed abstracts that mention MeSH 2026 drug, chemical, disease and organism records whose names never appear in 300,000 general-web documents. Queued; 3 seeds on SmolLM2-360M. Pre-registered prediction: a gain at least T4's size, below T5's. A null T7 would break the "gain follows novelty" story |
| The same on a second real domain | **T4** chemistry (ChEBI + real text): seeds 2–3 and the controls are training now. **T1c** clinical (MIMIC-III notes + SNOMED CT): queued |
| A downstream task, not only next-token loss | **T1c-F**: ICD diagnosis coding of rare codes from MIMIC-III discharge summaries, in the HRRBERT tradition (pre-registered, queued). This is the closest to a real application in the plan |
| A modern host on real text | **Not planned.** Qwen3 runs only on T5. **Recommendation:** if T7 is positive, add Qwen3-1.7B on T7 (about 15–20 GPU-h) |

**Direct conclusion.** The paper's headline has to come from T7, T4, T1c and T1c-F. There are three possible
outcomes:
- **Real-text gains hold (≥ 1–4% at 3 seeds on T7 and T4, plus a T1c-F coding gain on rare codes):** "it helps for
  new vocabulary" is a real-world claim, and T5 becomes the controlled upper bound.
- **Real-text gains are small or null:** the honest paper is an analysis paper. Ontology composition helps in
  proportion to how new the vocabulary is, and on real text that is little.
- **Independently of both:** the explicit-recall result (section 3.4) may become the main contribution.

## 3. Findings in plain terms

**Names used below.**
- **Host:** the pretrained model we fine-tune: SmolLM2-135M or 360M, or Qwen3-0.6B or 1.7B.
- **C0′:** the host fine-tuned on the domain text without the method. This is the fair baseline.
- **C5:** the host fine-tuned with the method.
- **C2:** a free lookup table with one learned vector per word. It has no ontology.
- **Held-out terms:** words whose vectors the model never trained on. The method builds them from the ontology alone.

### 3.1 On new words the method works (T5, synthetic)

| Host | After held-out terms | After unseen terms | After rare terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| SmolLM2-360M | **−13.3%** [−14.1, −12.4] | −6.6% | −6.0% |
| SmolLM2-135M | **−9.2%** [−10.0, −8.4] | −3.8% | −3.1% |
| Qwen3-1.7B (2 seeds) | **−17.7%** [−18.6, −16.8] | — | — |
| Qwen3-0.6B (2 seeds) | **−20.2%** [−21.3, −19.1] | — | — |

*Change in prediction loss vs C0′; negative = better.*

Text with no linked words is unchanged (within 0.2%), so the method does no harm elsewhere.

**Is this just copying?** Partly. 86–90% of the gain after rare and unseen terms falls on tokens that repeat a
related word from the ontology entry. After held-out terms only a third does. The other two thirds fall on other
tokens (−19.4%), so the model is not only copying.

### 3.2 The gain follows how new the vocabulary is

The column "how new" measures how much plain fine-tuning (C0′) lowers the host's loss on the domain's terms. A big
drop means the host did not know the vocabulary beforehand.

| Track | Text | How new (C0′ vs host) | Method's gain after held-out terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| T5 | synthetic glossary | −74% | −9 to −13% |
| T4 | real chemistry (seed 1) | −40 to −56% | −0.4 to −0.6% (unseen terms −2.3 to −3.7%) |
| T1-open | real MeSH / PubMed (seed 1) | −5 to −8% | ≈ 0 |
| WordNet | everyday English | −0.3 to −0.8% | 0 |

**Meaning:** the method fills a gap where the model lacks the word. On words the model already knows, fine-tuning
alone does the job and the composed vector adds nothing. T7 tests this on a second real domain (section 2).

### 3.3 What causes the gain: composition, not the binding operator

On T5 (SmolLM2-360M, 3 seeds), we swapped one part of the method at a time:

| Variant | vs C0′ on held-out terms | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| The method (HRR binding, learned) | −13.3% | — |
| Relations shuffled between words | **+4.6% (worse)** | the ontology content is necessary |
| Binding removed (untyped: a plain sum of related words) | same as the method (+0.2%, n.s.) | binding not needed for loss |
| Translation binding (relation added, not convolved) | **−17.2%** (4.8% better than the method) | a simpler operator is better |
| Fixed random binding (not learned) | −8.5% (5.2% worse than the method) | learning the operator helps in pretrained hosts |
| Vector from averaging the word's subtokens | +1.1% | standard new-word tricks do not reach it |
| Vector from encoding the word's definition | −2.8% | ″ |
| Vector from a knowledge-graph embedding (TransE) | +2.4% | ″ |

The method beats the three standard alternatives (last three rows) by 10.8–15.3%. **This is T5 only.**

**Update 9 October: the same controls on real chemistry text (T4, SmolLM2-360M, 3 seeds; R9 "T4 chemistry — seeds
1–3").** Change vs C0′ after held-out terms: the method −0.40%; shuffled relations −0.41%; fixed random binding −0.35%;
no binding −0.36%; translation −0.45%; subtoken mean −0.42%; definition encoder **−0.56%**; TransE −0.50%.
- The binding operator does not matter here either.
- After held-out terms the word's own relations do not matter: shuffled relations keep the whole gain. After seen,
  rare and never-seen terms they do (shuffling costs 0.82% after never-seen terms).
- After held-out terms the definition encoder beats the method (by 0.17% [0.05, 0.29]); the other two tie it.
- So on real text "a new word gets a useful vector for free from its relations" is not shown. What is shown is that
  the composed vector helps rare and unseen words whose relations it reads, but it is not the only way to do that: a
  frozen TransE vector of the entry ties it on every stratum, and the definition encoder ties it after rare and unseen
  terms. Composition beats only the subtoken mean. Decision 64 adds these controls to T7, T7-ROOD, T8 and T1c-ROOD.
- **Frame swap inside the trained models (9 October; `experiments/e9-retrofit/frameswap/`).** Giving a word another
  word's vector, no vector, or an average vector at evaluation time, inside the same trained model:
  - T5: the word's own vector matters a lot (another word's vector +24% loss after held-out terms, no vector +29%).
  - T4, rare words: the own vector is used (another word's vector +0.8%).
  - T4, words absent from the training text ("unseen"; 68% share no concept with any trained word, so their vectors
    are composed zero-shot): the own vector helps and is word-specific (another word's vector +1.3%, no vector +1.6%;
    135M +1.9% / +2.9%). **Zero-shot composition works on natural text for words the host never read.**
  - T4, held-out words: these are frequent words (≥ 5 occurrences) that stay in the training text unlinked, so the
    host learns them as plain tokens. Another word's vector is worse than their own (+0.19%), but **no vector is
    better than their own (−0.19%)**: injecting a vector for a word the host already learned without one costs a
    little. C5's held-out advantage over C0′ comes from the host weights trained with the channel. The definition
    encoder's held-out vector is neutral (no vector +0.01%), which accounts for its 0.17% edge over C5.
  - Still not shown on natural text: that composition beats other ways of encoding the same relations (TransE ties
    C5 on unseen and rare words).
  - T1 and WordNet: the host ignores the vector.
- **T7 (new 2025–26 names, SmolLM2-360M, 3 seeds; preliminary, 10 October).** C5 − C0′ after held-out names
  **+0.48% [+0.36, +0.61] (worse)**; after rare −0.50%*, after 3+-subtoken −0.57%*, inside −0.28%*, unlinked −0.01%.
  The pre-registered prediction (a gain at least T4's) fails on the primary stratum. T7's held-out names occur unlinked
  in its training text (the reason T7-ROOD exists), so this is T4's held-out situation with a larger cost. T7-ROOD,
  whose training text excludes them, is the clean zero-shot test; its C5sh and C6d controls are queued.
- **Frame swap over every linked term (10 October; `frameswap/<track>-linked/`).** Inside each trained model, after
  3+-subtoken terms: replacing every term's vector by another term's costs T4 +2.24%, T7 +1.00%, T1c (seed 1) +3.91%,
  T5 +34%; removing the vectors costs T4 +1.95%, T7 +0.97%, T1c +3.21%. The model relies on each term's own vector,
  more than the C5 − C0′ gain itself, because the host co-adapts to the channel; "no vector" is not a clean baseline.
  The shuffled-frames model shows the same pattern (T4 other +1.74%, T7 +0.85%): what the host relies on is a
  consistent, parameter-shared code per term. The ontology's actual relations add a smaller part, measured across models
  (C5 − C5sh after 3+-subtoken terms: T4 −0.20%*, T7 ≈ 0).

Trained from scratch at 50M parameters (the reduced operator screen), every composed variant beats the free lookup
table slightly: −0.13% to −0.25% after held-out terms and −1.6% to −2.1% after rare terms. All binding operators tie.

**Meaning:** the right name for the mechanism is *compositional parameter sharing*. Words share learned vectors for
their related concepts, and a new word gets a useful vector for free. The HRR binding is not what makes next-token
prediction better.

### 3.4 Does the binding store anything the model can use? (binding program and E12; owned by the peer session)

- **The stored relations can be read back.** Unbinding a word's vector recovers its related words almost perfectly:
  0.99 mean reciprocal rank on held-out words, against 0.21 from guessing by frequency.
- **Learning the binding adds nothing here.** A fixed random binding stores and decodes the relations as well or
  slightly better: P2 learned − fixed −0.006 [−0.016, +0.003]; hop 1 0.97 vs 0.94; reverse lookup 0.98 vs 0.96.
- **The model does not use them on its own.** We built word pairs whose relations are swapped (A *treats* X and is
  *caused by* Y; B the reverse). Every model scores chance at telling them apart (0.48–0.50), with or without
  binding. This pre-registered test (P1) is null.
- **Chained and reverse lookups work only from a per-word store. Binding beats the simpler operators only where a
  role is ambiguous, or against translation.**
  - Two-hop chains through per-word vectors: 0.88 correct. Through one global superposed memory: 0.07.
  - Reverse lookup with ambiguous fillers (one related word under two roles): HRR beats untyped by 0.38 and
    translation by 0.19.
  - Two-hop chains: HRR beats translation by 0.11. It does **not** beat untyped (+0.011 [−0.015, +0.037], p 0.29),
    because in these items each related word mostly identifies its role, so a plain sum chains as well.
  - Word order along a path is not encoded by any arm (0.49–0.50). This is expected: circulant binding is
    commutative. A non-commutative operator (block-diagonal unitary) is tested with the readout arms (≈ 13–14 Oct).
- **Decoding the store into the prompt makes the model use it** (E12 Q1, pre-registered, SmolLM2-360M, 3 seeds; report
  `experiments/e12-self-query/report/t5-q1-f1/`). Accuracy on the swapped pairs:

  | Context given to the model | Accuracy |
  |---|---|
  | Nothing | 0.50 |
  | Its HRR store, decoded to text | **0.78** |
  | The same decode from an untyped store | 0.50 |
  | The same decode from a translation store | 0.49 |
  | The gold relations as text | 0.86 |
  | A definition in prose | 0.74 |

  Both primary contrasts pass with Holm correction:
  - recall − nothing: +0.28 [+0.26, +0.30];
  - HRR store − untyped store: +0.27 [+0.24, +0.30].

  It works just as well for the plain fine-tuned model reading the HRR store (0.79; the difference from C5 is
  −0.01, n.s.), so the roles live in the store, not in the host. Removing one decoded relation from the store changes the
  answer as expected (faithfulness F1: +0.36 [+0.33, +0.39]).
- **The same holds on real chemistry text** (T4, seed 1, natural role-ambiguous items). Accuracy:

  | Context given to the model | Accuracy |
  |---|---|
  | Nothing | 0.55 |
  | Decoded from the HRR store | **0.88** |
  | Decoded from an untyped store | 0.50 |
  | Decoded from a translation store | 0.51 |
  | The gold relations as text | 0.89 |
  | A definition in prose | 0.91 |

  Seeds 2–3 are queued.

**Meaning:** "VSA" earns its place where a relation must be read out explicitly and the role is ambiguous: decoding,
reverse lookup, explanation. It does not earn it in next-token loss. Learning the operator does not help read-out: a
fixed random binding is as good.

The pre-registered 3-seed recall result (Q1) **passed**. With the seed-1 replication on real chemistry text, it is now
the main evidence for the VSA framing. On that real text, a plain definition in the prompt does about as well (0.91),
so the store's advantage is that it needs no curated definition and costs fewer tokens; accuracy is not the
advantage.

### 3.5 Quantization (4-bit)

- **The gain survives but does not grow.** After 4-bit compression the method keeps 0.58× (360M) to 1.14× (135M) of
  its held-out-term advantage on T5.
- **It depends on the quantizer.** GPTQ keeps it fully; plain rounding loses part of it.
- **The extra robustness is in the host weights.** The method's models degrade less on ordinary domain text, but that
  comes from how the host weights were trained, not from the added vector: switching the vector off leaves it.
- **On real chemistry text the gain shrinks under 4 bits.**

**Meaning:** no "makes quantized models more robust" claim. At most one hedged sentence.

### 3.6 Editing knowledge by editing the ontology (claim C)

- **T5:** writing an ontology entry for a new word improves a property-selection test from 0.19 to 0.26 (chance
  0.20). Qwen3 improves by 9–16 points.
- **T4 real chemistry:** no effect.
- **Editing one relation of a known word** shifts predictions toward the new fact (+0.69 log-odds at seed 1).

**Meaning:** a proof of concept on synthetic data only. "Edit a symbolic store instead of weights" is already
published; only this specific mechanism is new.

### 3.7 Self-learning (E10)

- **Synthetic ontologies.** The learnable ontology recovers erased relations. Standard rule-mining (AMIE) finds the
  same things, and the acceptance test wrongly accepts structure in worlds that have none.
- **WordNet with a frozen GPT-2.** Nothing transfers.
- **The active + passive version (E10.9a)** is not faster than passive learning at matched data (difference −0.003
  [−0.019, +0.013]). It does write useful rule-implied relations for concepts never observed (+0.021 at full data).
  In a world with no hidden structure, wrong commitments are harmless but never retracted.

**Meaning:** no self-learning claim in the paper. At most a synthetic methods section. The follow-up (E10.9b) is
not run.

### 3.8 Early experiments on frozen models (E1, E2, E3, F.1)

Fitting composed vectors to a frozen model's existing word vectors did not work:
- HRR was no better than simpler operators (F.1 refuted).
- Sense alignment fell below the most-frequent-sense baseline (E1 gate fail).
- WordNet sense recovery failed (E3 gate fail).

These results moved the program to joint training, where the results above come from. They belong in the paper as
motivation, not as claims.

## 4. Conclusions

1. **Claim:** composed ontology vectors give a pretrained model a usable representation for words it has never seen,
   and the benefit grows with how unfamiliar the vocabulary is. Today this is shown on synthetic data and weakly on
   one real domain.
2. **Do not claim:** that the VSA/HRR binding improves language modelling. Simpler operators match or beat it.
3. **Claim (3 seeds on T5; seed 1 on real chemistry text):** the binding gives an explicitly readable store. Decoding
   it into the prompt lets a model resolve relations it otherwise cannot. HRR beats the untyped and translation operators where roles
   are ambiguous. Learned and fixed binding perform the same for decoding and queries, and for loss when training
   from scratch. Only for a pretrained host's loss on T5 did learning the operator help (by 5.2%), and translation
   helped more.
4. **Do not claim:** quantization robustness, self-learning speed-ups, or editing on real text.
5. **The manuscript cannot rest on T5.** Its fate depends on T7, T4 (3 seeds), T1c and the ICD-coding task, all due
   by about 12 October.
